# Part 1 - The menstrual cycle and training, where the evidence is weak

This is the topic that gets the most attention and supports the least confident advice. Reporting
that accurately is more useful than repeating the popular version.

## What the reviews actually found

- **Strength: no meaningful effect.** Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of the influence of the
  menstrual cycle on force production indicate **menstrual cycle phase has no meaningful effect on
  force production capabilities.**
- **Resistance training adaptations: no established effect.** It is described as **premature to
  conclude that short-term fluctuations in reproductive hormones appreciably influence acute exercise
  performance or longer-term strength or hypertrophic adaptations.**
- **Performance generally: small and inconsistent.** In elite athletes, various performance
  parameters are affected across the cycle, but **the parameters themselves and the magnitude and
  direction of the effects are inconclusive.** The most recent meta-analysis suggests performance
  might be only **trivially** reduced in the early follicular phase compared with other phases.
- **The evidence base is poor.** Reviews consistently emphasise low study quality and inconsistent
  methodology, particularly in how cycle phases were verified. The most recent meta-analysis states
  outright that **current evidence cannot be used to develop exercise guidelines.**

## What follows from that

**Do not build a training plan around cycle phases.** Phase-based periodisation - heavy in the
follicular phase, lighter in the luteal - is a widely marketed idea that the current evidence does
not support. Writing it into a program would be dogma dressed as science, which is exactly what
`coaching-method.md` opens by warning against.

**What is legitimate is entirely different**, and worth separating carefully:

- **Symptoms are real even where average performance effects are not.** Pain, heavy bleeding,
  fatigue and disrupted sleep affect training on the days they happen, regardless of what a group
  mean says. An athlete adjusting a session because she feels bad that day is responding to
  information, not to a hormone model.
- **That is already how this project handles everything else.** `limiters()` exists precisely so the
  athlete's own account of what is holding a session back overrides the coach's reading of the
  numbers. A cycle-related limiter would be handled identically to "Zone 2 is a walk for me" - stated
  by her, in her words, overriding anything here.
- **Heavy menstrual bleeding is a direct iron-status risk factor**, and that link is much better
  evidenced than the performance one. See Part 2.

**So: nothing to program, one thing to make possible.** The useful action is that the existing
limiter mechanism already accommodates this if she wants to use it, and nobody should ask her to.

# Part 2 - Iron, where the evidence is strong and the risk is real

## Prevalence

- **Female athletes, endurance athletes and athletes losing weight are all elevated-risk groups** for
  iron deficiency. Cerys is arguably in all three.
- Estimates of iron deficiency prevalence in exercising women run **as high as 57%** in some
  populations, and around **40% of adolescent female athletes** are iron deficient, **less than half
  of whom are anaemic** - which is the crucial detail, because it means a normal haemoglobin does not
  rule it out.

## Why athletes lose more of it

Iron loss is exaggerated in athletes through **haemolysis from repeated foot strike, sweating and
gastrointestinal bleeding**. Menstruating athletes lose additional iron each cycle and consequently
have higher dietary iron requirements.

Note the mechanism in that list: **foot-strike haemolysis**. Running specifically, as opposed to
cycling or rowing, is the modality that drives it.

## Thresholds

- **Ferritin below 15 ng/mL** carried a 52-fold greater likelihood ratio of iron deficiency in a
  systematic review.
- **15-35 ng/mL is predictive of low iron stores** - which is why an athlete can be told her bloods
  are "normal" and still be functionally deficient. Ferritin is the test that matters; a full blood
  count alone will miss it.
- Ferritin screening or nutritional iron screening is a standard recommendation for incoming
  athletes in collegiate settings.

## Why this belongs in a file about Cerys's training

The symptom picture of iron deficiency - **fatigue, poor recovery, effort feeling harder than the
pace justifies** - is indistinguishable from under-recovery and from under-fuelling by observation
alone. It is a blood test, and it is the kind of thing that quietly costs someone six months.

**Iron is also relevant to bone**: it is required for collagen production and bone remodelling. The
direct evidence linking low ferritin to bone stress injuries is described as still emerging, but the
biological link is plausible and sits inside the female athlete triad.

**The action is not a supplement.** Iron supplementation without a test is a bad idea - excess iron
is harmful and the dose depends on the deficit. **The action is a blood test including ferritin, if
and when she wants one**, and it is the single most informative test available to her.

# Part 3 - Bone stress, energy availability and the triad

## The triad, and why it is one thing rather than three

**Low energy availability, menstrual dysfunction and reduced bone mineral density** are linked, and
low energy availability is the underlying driver of the other two. The broader RED-S framing
generalises this beyond bone and beyond women.

- Energy availability below roughly **30 kcal/kg fat-free mass per day** is the commonly used
  female threshold. It derives from laboratory studies of non-athletic women in the early 2000s
  where menstrual disturbances appeared below it, and **its universal applicability is disputed** -
  individual and sex differences are large.
- **Bone stress injury incidence rises with the number of female athlete triad risk factors
  present.** That is the finding that connects this part to the shins.
- Lower bone mineral density is observed in female elite athletes with menstrual dysfunction across
  mixed sports.
- Low energy availability produces **stress fractures and tendon injuries through weakened tissue and
  immune changes**, alongside fatigue and mood disturbance.

## Applied carefully to what we actually know (2026-09-02)

The facts, and only the facts:

- Cerys has **one bodyweight on record ever**: 79.8 kg, 1 August 2026. There is no trend, so nothing
  can be said about energy availability in either direction.
- She has **medial tibial stress syndrome**, which is a bone-stress-spectrum condition, and it has
  flared repeatedly.
- She has flagged **three joints in six weeks** - shins, left hip, right shoulder.
- There is **no wellness data, no nutritional information, and no bloods.**

**What that does and does not license:**

- It **does not** license any suggestion that she is under-fuelled, has low energy availability, or
  has a triad component. There is no evidence of any of that and asserting it would be both wrong
  and harmful.
- It **does** license saying that a recurrent bone-stress injury in a female endurance athlete is a
  presentation where the triad is on the list of things worth ruling out, that ruling it out is
  cheap, and that we currently have none of the information that would.

The framing that respects both: **"here is a category of explanation we cannot currently see, and
here is the cheap way to see it"** - a few weigh-ins, overnight watch wear, and bloods including
ferritin if she wants them. That is the same posture the rest of this document set takes toward
Daniel's tiredness notes.

# Part 4 - The first pull-up

Cerys's only stated strength goal, and the thing she is most obviously succeeding at.

## Where she actually is (2026-08-29)

**47.8 kg effective and rising** - bodyweight minus the machine's assistance - having gone from 36 kg
of assist down to 32 kg. At 79.8 kg bodyweight, she is lifting about **60% of herself** and needs to
reach 100%.

Two things worth saying plainly, because her notes under-record what goes well:

- **This is the strongest strength trend either of them has**, in the sense of moving fastest toward
  its stated goal.
- **The app got this wrong until August** and showed the coach 36 kg and falling. Do not re-read the
  raw first column on an assisted lift; `loadTypeOf`/`setLoad` handle it.

## What the evidence supports

- **Eccentric work is the highest-yield method.** Eccentric contractions produce higher force than
  concentric, and eccentric-based training produced **greater strength gains, hypertrophy and tendon
  stiffness**. In a randomised trial in women comparing concentric-only, eccentric-only and combined
  training, **eccentric-based training retained both eccentric and concentric strength**, while
  concentric-based training retained only concentric gains. That retention property matters for
  someone whose training keeps getting interrupted by injuries elsewhere.
- **Negatives plus band-assisted pull-ups, 2-3 times a week**, is the commonly cited practical
  protocol, with first unassisted rep often arriving in 4-8 weeks - though that figure comes from
  practitioner sources rather than controlled trials, so treat it as encouraging rather than
  predictive.
- The assisted machine she is already using is a legitimate tool and the progression is already
  working. The evidence argues for **adding eccentrics**, not for replacing what she does.

## Two things that make this the right goal to push right now

1. **It needs neither shins nor hips.** `coaching-method.md` Part 3 already says this: when something
   in the lower body is injured, the pull-up is an opportunity rather than a lost week. That override
   is well founded and should be used more aggressively than it has been.
2. **It transfers to Hyrox.** The sled pull is a pulling station under grip load, and grip is a named
   co-limiter across two stations (see `hyrox-method.md` Part 3). Her pull-up work is not competing
   with the Hyrox goal.

## Bodyweight and the pull-up, handled honestly

Relative strength is the ratio of strength to bodyweight, and both terms move it. **That is a true
statement and it is not a licence to suggest anyone lose weight.** Given the bone-stress and energy
availability material in Part 3, and given that we have exactly one weigh-in, the responsible
position is:

- **Bodyweight is not a lever anyone should be reaching for here.** The numerator is moving fine.
- The reason to record bodyweight more often is **so the pull-up scores correctly and so a trend
  exists if it is ever needed**, not so it can be reduced.
- If she raises it herself, that is different, and it belongs with a professional rather than with a
  coaching note.

# Part 5 - What is actually actionable

Everything here is optional, personal, and hers to decide. None of it is a coaching note.

1. **Bloods including ferritin**, if she wants them. Highest information per unit of effort of
   anything in this file, and the one where "normal" results are routinely misread.
2. **Weigh in more than once.** It unblocks correct pull-up scoring, which is her own goal.
3. **Overnight watch wear**, which is in `recovery-method.md` and applies to both of them.
4. **Add eccentrics to the pull-up work** - the one place this file makes a training suggestion, and
   it goes through `propose_suggestion_tool` like any other program change.

   to record a cycle-related limiter, `limiters()` already handles it in her own words.
