# Fuelling method - energy, protein, carbohydrate, and the notes that prompted this

**Written 2026-09-02.**

## Why this file exists

Daniel's session notes carry a repeated pattern: "feel tired", "low energy", "ended early -
hungry". Over the same period his bodyweight went from 78.4 kg (5 July) to 74.6 kg (12 August), a
**3.8 kg drop in five and a half weeks**, while he was training five days a week and chasing a
200 kg squat and a 200 kg deadlift.

Nobody has ever looked at whether he is eating enough. There is no food data, no wellness data and
no way to know. This file is the evidence base for that question, so that when it does get asked
it gets asked properly.

## Two rules before anything below is used

1. **This is not a diagnosis and it is not clinical advice.** `coaching-method.md` Part 3 says the
   coach does not diagnose, and that applies with more force here than anywhere else. Low energy
   availability, iron status and disordered eating are medical territory. The right output of this
   file is a flagged observation and "worth getting checked", never a conclusion.
2. **Numbers below are population guidance, and individuals vary widely.** They are for setting a
   sensible starting point and noticing when something is far outside the range, not for precision.

# Part 1 - Energy availability, which is the one that matters here

## The concept

**Energy availability** is energy taken in, minus the energy burned in exercise, divided by
fat-free mass. It is what is left over to run everything that is not training: hormones, bone
remodelling, immune function, mood, sleep, digestion.

It is not the same as "eating enough to maintain weight". Weight can be stable while availability is
low, and weight can drop for benign reasons. But a **sustained drop in bodyweight during a period of
high training load, accompanied by fatigue and hunger notes, is the textbook presentation** of it.

## The thresholds

- The commonly cited cut-off is **30 kcal/kg fat-free mass per day** for females. It comes from
  laboratory studies of non-athletic women in the early 2000s, where menstrual disturbances appeared
  below it, and **its universal applicability is genuinely disputed**.
- If a male-specific threshold exists it is **lower - proposals sit around 9-25 kcal/kg FFM/day**,
  with male physiology apparently more resistant. Assessment commonly uses **below 25** for males and
  **below 30** for females.
- Treat these as rough flags, not diagnostic lines.

## What low energy availability produces

This is the list worth knowing, because several items on it are things Daniel and Cerys have
actually logged:

- **Fatigue and mood disturbance** - the "feel tired", "low energy" notes.
- **Injuries, specifically stress fractures and tendon injuries**, through weakened tissue and
  altered bone remodelling. Cerys's shins and the three joints flagged in six weeks sit
  uncomfortably close to this.
- **Loss of fat-free mass** - which is the opposite of what a 200 kg squat needs.
- Endocrine changes, suppression of the reproductive axis, thyroid suppression, altered metabolic
  response, gastrointestinal and cardiovascular dysfunction, and a picture that looks exactly like
  overtraining.

**The overlap with overtraining is the trap.** "Tired, flat, sessions ending early, performance
stalling" reads as too much training. It reads identically as not enough food. The two get opposite
prescriptions, and there is currently no data on either of them that would tell the two apart.

## Applied to Daniel

Facts, without inference beyond them:

- 78.4 kg to 74.6 kg between 5 July and 12 August. No weigh-in since 12 August.
- Five training days a week across that period.
- Repeated notes of tiredness, low energy, and ending a session early because of hunger.
- Strength goals (100 kg bench, 200 kg squat, 200 kg deadlift) that require building tissue, which
  requires an energy surplus or at minimum not a deficit.
- **He has an 85 kg bodyweight figure on file as a background item, not a live goal.** It is
  mentioned here only because it is the opposite direction of travel, not to reopen it. Whether
  bodyweight is something he wants to move is his call and nobody has asked.

**The honest read:** losing 3.8 kg while training five days a week and reporting hunger and fatigue
is consistent with under-fuelling, and it is *also* consistent with several benign explanations
(a deliberate change, a hydration or scale artefact given eleven of the thirteen weigh-ins read an
identical 77.2 kg, a change in circumstances). **The right next step is data, not advice**: more
frequent weigh-ins, and some sense of what he actually eats on a training day.

## Applied to Cerys

**One bodyweight on record, ever: 79.8 kg on 1 August.** That is not enough to say anything at all,
and it also has a concrete downstream cost already documented in the project: her pull-up could not
be scored correctly until a bodyweight existed, because assisted-lift scoring needs it.

The relevant risks for her sit in `female-athlete-method.md` - energy availability interacts with
bone stress risk and with iron status, and she is the one with a bone-stress-adjacent injury.

# Part 2 - Protein

## The numbers

- **Position-stand range for athletes doing resistance and endurance training: 1.2-2.0 g/kg/day**
  (Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics / Dietitians of Canada / ACSM).
- **Dose-response meta-analysis:** muscle strength gains rise with total protein intake at about
  **0.72% per additional 0.1 g/kg/day, up to roughly 1.5 g/kg/day**, and flatten after that.
- **Protein supplementation beyond about 1.62 g/kg/day produced no further gains in fat-free mass.**
- Endurance-specific work using the indicator amino acid oxidation method landed higher than older
  recommendations, at around **1.83 g/kg/day for endurance-trained adults**.

## The practical read

**1.6 g/kg/day is a sensible target for both of them**, comfortably inside every range above and
past the point where the strength dose-response flattens. Going much beyond 2.0 g/kg/day is not
supported by the evidence for either goal.

At current bodyweights that is roughly:

- **Daniel (74.6 kg): about 120 g/day.**
- **Cerys (79.8 kg): about 128 g/day.**

Distribution across meals was not well covered by the sources reviewed here, so no claim is made
about it. Total daily intake is the well-supported variable.

**The caveat that matters more than the number:** protein cannot fix an energy deficit. If total
intake is too low, protein gets burned for fuel rather than used for tissue. Energy comes first,
protein second.

# Part 3 - Carbohydrate

## Daily intake

For moderate-to-high intensity training of roughly an hour a day - which is what both of their weeks
look like - the guidance is **5-7 g/kg/day**.

At current bodyweights: **Daniel around 375-520 g/day, Cerys around 400-560 g/day.** Those are large
numbers and they routinely surprise people. They also scale down on lighter days; carbohydrate is
the macronutrient that should move with the training load rather than sitting fixed.

## Around a session

- **1-4 g/kg in the 1-4 hours before exercise** for sessions over 60 minutes. Three to four hours
  out, 1-2 g/kg is the standard figure.
- **Keep the pre-session meal low in fat and fibre.** Fat delays gastric emptying, which is what
  produces the heavy, sloshing feeling in a session that started too soon after eating.
- **After: 0.75-1.5 g/kg as soon as practical** to maximise glycogen resynthesis. This matters most
  when the next session is within about 24 hours, which for a five-day training week it usually is.

## The specific relevance to "ended early - hungry"

A session that ends early because of hunger is a session that started under-fuelled. The two
cheapest fixes, in order:

1. **A carbohydrate-containing meal 2-3 hours before**, not fat- or fibre-heavy.
2. **Something small and fast-acting in the 20-30 minutes before**, if the gap since the last meal
   is long.

Neither requires knowing anything about total intake, and either would resolve the symptom if the
symptom is what it looks like. If it does not resolve it, that is itself informative and points back
at Part 1.

# Part 4 - Supplements, ranked by how much the evidence actually supports them

Only four are worth discussing. Everything else is noise at their level.

## Creatine monohydrate - the strongest evidence of any sports supplement

- **Consistently ergogenic** for high-intensity exercise capacity and lean mass. The ISSN describes
  it as one of the very few nutritional supplements with consistently demonstrated benefit.
- Safety: up to 30 g/day for five years shows no adverse effects in healthy and clinical
  populations. Standard maintenance dosing is far below that.
- **Relevance here:** it targets exactly the repeated high-intensity efforts that make up a Hyrox
  station and a set of squats. It is the most defensible thing on this list.

## Caffeine - well-evidenced, easy to get wrong

- **3-6 mg/kg improves performance in trained athletes; higher doses add nothing** and add side
  effects. Minimum effective dose may be as low as 2 mg/kg.
- **Timing: around 60 minutes before**, and anhydrous caffeine is more ergogenic than coffee.
- **Aerobic endurance is the exercise type with the most consistent moderate-to-large benefit**, at
  roughly 2-3% in time-trial tasks - which is meaningful over 8 km of Hyrox running or a 5k.
- At 74.6 kg and 79.8 kg, 3-6 mg/kg is roughly 225-450 mg and 240-480 mg respectively. The low end
  of that is one strong coffee; the high end is a lot, and individual response varies enormously.

## Beta-alanine - real, narrow, and probably not for them

- **3.2-6.4 g/day for at least 2-4 weeks**, split into 0.8-1.6 g doses to limit the tingling.
- The benefit is most pronounced in **open-ended tasks lasting 1-4 minutes.**
- **More research is needed on strength and on endurance beyond 25 minutes** - which is most of what
  they do. A Hyrox station lasts roughly the right duration, so it is not irrelevant, but it is a
  much weaker case than creatine.
- No synergy with creatine was found when the two were studied together.

## Everything else

Not covered here, deliberately. If a specific supplement comes up, research it then rather than
carrying a stale list.

# Part 5 - Race day, when a race exists

Untested on race day is a mistake. **Everything in this section gets rehearsed in a training
session or a race simulation first** - see `hyrox-method.md` Phase 4.

- **Carb loading:** around **8 g/kg of carbohydrate in the 24 hours before**, from easily digested
  sources - rice, oats, bread, fruit. Not a single enormous pasta meal.
- **Breakfast: a carbohydrate-rich meal 2.5-3 hours before the start**, low in fat and fibre.
- **Top-up:** something small and fast-acting 20-30 minutes before.
- **During:** for a 75-120 minute effort, **30-60 g of carbohydrate per hour** plus electrolytes. For
  a 90-minute race that is roughly a gel at 25 minutes and another at 50, with an optional third
  around 75.
- **Practise gels in training.** Gastrointestinal distress on race day is common and entirely
  avoidable, and it is the single most likely way for a well-trained pair to have a bad day.

Note the doubles wrinkle: **the pair must stay within 10 seconds of each other**, so anything that
takes one person out of action takes both. Fuelling problems are a team problem in doubles.

# Part 6 - What to actually do next

In order of value, and all of it is data-gathering rather than intervention. None of this is a
coaching note; the ones that change anything are proposals for Daniel.

1. **Cerys weighs in.** One data point from 1 August is not a trend, and it also blocks correct
   scoring of her pull-up, which is her stated goal.
2. **Daniel weighs in regularly enough to see a trend** - the app already imports scale CSVs. Eleven
   of his thirteen weigh-ins read an identical 77.2 kg, which suggests the data is not being
   captured as often as it looks.
3. **Establish roughly what a training day's eating looks like**, once, for both. Not a food diary
   forever - a snapshot that says whether the numbers in Parts 2 and 3 are being met within an order
   of magnitude.
4. **Fix the pre-session meal before concluding anything about total intake.** It is the cheapest
   possible explanation for "ended early - hungry" and it costs nothing to test.
5. **If fatigue persists once fuelling around sessions is sorted, get it checked.** Bloods, including
   iron - see `female-athlete-method.md` for why that matters particularly for Cerys.
