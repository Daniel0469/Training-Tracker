# Flexibility method - the ladder, and why cool-downs stopped being stretching sessions

This is the reasoning behind the Mobility assessment session, the Flexibility pane in Progress,
and the warm-up / cool-down rules in `coaching-method.md` Part 3. Those three changes only make
sense together, and they all come from one method.

**Source.** Matt Smith (`matthewismith` on YouTube), *The Blueprint to Get Flexible Once, Forever*
- <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3j7oSqCuTHs>, 39 minutes, reviewed 2026-09-02 from Daniel's
transcript. This is a summary in our own words for our own use; the transcript itself is not
copied into the repo. Go to the video for the demonstrations, which are most of its value - the
side-split hip tilt and the shoulder band isolations are much clearer shown than described.

Treat everything below as a coherent system rather than a menu. The scheduling section is the part
that changed our program most, and it is the part most people skip.

---

## 1. The flexibility ladder

Everything that restricts a position sits at some level: individual muscles, nerve tension,
technique, and strength in the range. **Your lowest rung is your bottleneck**, and it is the only
thing worth training. Raising anything above it changes nothing.

The failure mode this replaces is stretching whatever feels tight, or whatever is conventionally
blamed - hamstrings for a toe touch, lats for an overhead reach. What you *feel* is not reliably
what is *limiting*, which is why the method is assess-first and why guessing is treated as the
amateur path rather than a shortcut.

So the loop is: find the lowest rung, fix it, find the next lowest, repeat until the position is
available.

## 2. Skills, not routines

"Hip mobility" is as meaningless a goal as "hip strength". In the gym nobody trains hip strength;
they train a squat or a deadlift. Flexibility goals should be movement patterns for the same reason.

The eight skills: **pike** (touching your toes), **pancake**, **side split**, **front split**,
**bridge**, **shoulder flexion**, **shoulder extension**, and a **deep squat**.

Train one or two at a time, not all of them. They overlap, so the others do not rot while you
work: pike feeds the front leg of a front split, and bridge feeds the back leg. Stretching a
muscle in isolation also does not guarantee you can *use* the range once other tissues, nerves
and coordination are involved - which is why the compound skill is the unit, the same way a
back squat beats a leg extension.

## 3. Assessment

Test tissues **in isolation** to find which one actually stops the position.

- **Overhead reach.** Take the arm behind the body to isolate pec major; externally rotate the
  shoulder to isolate subscapularis and teres major; round the lower back to isolate the lats,
  which are the only ones of the group crossing the lumbar spine.
- **Toe touch.** Not just hamstrings - also adductor magnus, gastrocnemius, sciatic nerve tension,
  and the *strength* of the hip and spine flexors that have to pull you in.
- **Side split.** Tailor's pose against a wall (can the calves reach the floor?) for the short
  adductors and hip rotators with a bent knee; the pancake hip tilt (head to floor with a rounded
  back) for whether the pelvis can tilt forward at all.

**Passive vs isometric vs active is the key comparison.** Two people at the same depth can need
opposite programs: the one with lots of passive range and little active control needs strength into
the range they own, while the one with little range and good control needs more total range.

## 4. Stretch stacking

Pick **3-5 stretches aimed at the same skill**, and order them **small and isolated first,
compound last** - the reverse of strength training, where the big lift goes first while you are
fresh.

The point is that each one opens range for the next: free the pec, and the lat stretch goes deeper
because the pec is no longer the limit. Two stretches for the same area are worth more than one
each for two areas. This multiplying effect is the whole argument against full-body routines.

## 5. The assisted-to-resisted spectrum

Five zones, from **heavily assisted** (body weight and gravity driving you deeper), through
**assisted**, **neutral**, and **resisted**, to **heavily resisted** (loaded strength work through
your existing range).

Assisted work pushes the ceiling of total range. Resisted work makes the range strong and usable.
Alternating them stacks in both directions: the assisted exercise opens range for the resisted one
to strengthen, and the resisted one contracts the antagonist, which relaxes the target so the
assisted one goes deeper next round.

Ten methods live on that spectrum:

1. **Post-isometric relaxation** - contract, then relax deeper. Repeated for reps.
2. **Isometric stretching** - contract the muscle being stretched, holding yourself in position.
3. **Isometric holds for reps** - roughly 3s per rep with short rests, rather than one 30s hold.
   Each rep goes deeper because of the rest, so the stimulus beats the long hold. One of his most
   frequently programmed methods.
4. **Active lifts** - contract the opposite muscles to pull yourself in. Eccentric overload works
   here: lift higher with a bent knee, straighten, then lower.
5. **Dynamic stretching** - speed and momentum, for transferring range to fast movement.
6. **Ballistic / rapid contract-relax** - pulsing. He prefers RCR, using your own muscles rather
   than momentum, so you keep control of the depth.
7. **Joint capsule releases** - for restrictions that are not muscular and do not respond to
   ordinary stretching.
8. **Nerve flosses, glides and tensioners** - nerves stretch only about 8-10% beyond resting
   length and otherwise slide in their sheath. An entrapment can stop progress with no pain at all.
9. **Soft tissue work** - targeted, and used *through* the stretch rather than as blind rolling.
10. **Technique drills** - the most underrated. A side split fails on hip jam until you anteriorly
    tilt the pelvis or externally rotate the legs; sometimes technique is the only missing rung.

## 6. Scheduling - the part that changed our program

**Flexibility training is training, not recovery.** The mechanism is different from strength work
but the endpoint is the same: stretch opens calcium channels, calcium accumulates, calpains
activate, fibres are damaged, inflammation follows, and the brain reads it as fatigue. It arrives
hours later and clears over days, exactly like lifting.

That matters because fatigue signals reaching the brain **reduce stretch tolerance**, and stretch
tolerance is where the lasting adaptation happens. Training flexibility while fatigued spends the
session and gets less of the thing you came for.

His ranking, best to worst:

1. **2+ days after** the sport or strength work that loads the same area.
2. **Same day** - strength in the morning, flexibility in the evening. Works because the fatigue
   signal has not reached the brain yet.
3. **Straight after** the strength session, with the volume of both trimmed a little.
4. **Worst, and the common mistake:** the day *after* strength training, or parked on a rest day
   as "active recovery". It is not recovery.

**3-5 exercises, 1-2 sessions a week, 1-2 skills at a time.** That is the whole prescription.

## 7. Why it lasts

Two kinds of adaptation:

- **Nervous system** - stretch tolerance, changed at a supraspinal level. Durable for *years*.
- **Tissue** - sarcomeres added in series, tendon stiffness. Reverses after roughly **six weeks**
  of disuse.

Most programs optimise for the second and lose it. Worse, most stretching is really just warming
up: it changes the viscoelastic properties of muscle and tendon, gives a few centimetres, and is
gone in hours. That is fine for a warm-up and useless as training - and it is why our assessment
insists on measuring **cold**.

Hence "stretch right, stretch once": a season of focused work, then it is surprisingly cheap to
keep. This is also why a warm-up in our program is now explicitly described as preparation for
today rather than as something that builds flexibility.

## 8. What we have applied, and what is still open

**Applied (2026-09-02):**

- **The Mobility assessment session** - nine tests, fourteen measurements, floor- or wall-referenced
  so they repeat. Covers the pike cluster (standing fold, passive and active straight-leg raise),
  the shared pelvic-tilt rung (pancake), the deep-squat cluster (Tailor's pose, knee-to-wall, heel
  lift) and a shoulder baseline. Measured cold, day unassigned.
- **The Flexibility pane in Progress** - the ladder as a table: every rung, where it sits, which way
  it has moved, worse side reported for two-sided tests.
- **Cool-downs stopped being flexibility sessions.** PAILs/RAILs came out of the cardio day, and the
  core and glute work came out of both Lower days. See `coaching-method.md` Part 3 for the full list
  and the instruction not to write any of it back.

**Two departures from the source, both deliberate:**

- **Knee-to-wall** is not in the video. It is the standard ankle dorsiflexion test and the most
  common hard stop on squat depth, so it earns a place on a deep-squat ladder. Same principle:
  isolate the tissue, measure it.
- **Daniel chose pike + deep squat** as the first pair rather than one lower and one upper. They
  share adductors, the calf group and the pelvic tilt, so they stack better than a lower/upper
  split would - at the cost of leaving Cerys's pull-up goal for the following block.

**Open:**

- **Which skill leads**, and **which day the session gets** - both waiting on the uni timetable and
  on the two of them actually doing the assessment.
- **No mobility *training* session exists yet** - only the assessment. The 3-5 stacked exercises get
  built from real numbers, not guessed in advance. That is the whole point of the ladder.

## 9. What the assessment numbers should be

Section 3 says how to test. It does not say what a result means, which makes a measurement hard to
act on - a number with no reference is a number you can only compare against yourself later.

These are population reference values, not targets. **Their own first measurement is the reference
that actually matters**, because the method is about finding *your* lowest rung, not about reaching
someone else's. Use these to spot a genuinely restricted rung, not to grade anyone.

### Knee-to-wall (ankle dorsiflexion)

The best-referenced test on the list, and the one most likely to be the hard stop on a deep squat.

| Distance, toe to wall | Read as |
|---|---|
| under 4 cm | **Clinically significant restriction** |
| 4-9 cm | Borderline, subclinical restriction |
| 10 cm or more | Normal |
| 15 cm and up | Typical of young athletes |

Reported normal ranges by sex sit around **12.1-13.9 cm for women** and **13.6-14.9 cm for men**;
older or sedentary populations average 8-10 cm.

**The left/right comparison is as informative as the absolute number.** Our assessment already
reports the worse side for two-sided tests, which is the right call.

### Shoulder flexion (the overhead baseline)

Normal active forward flexion is **160-180 degrees**. Median active flexion at ages 30-34 is about
**170 degrees for men and 166 for women**. Below about 160 is where an overhead position starts
costing you something.

### Squat depth

Standard operational definitions, useful because "deep squat" is otherwise undefined:

- **Partial or shallow:** 0-90 degrees of knee flexion.
- **Medium:** 90-110 degrees, roughly thigh parallel to the floor.
- **Full or deep:** 110-135 degrees.

The Hyrox wall ball standard needs at least the medium band, held for 100 reps under fatigue - see
section 11.

### The measurement rules that already exist and still apply

- **Measure cold.** Section 7 explains why: a warm measurement is measuring viscoelastic change,
  which is gone in hours and is not the adaptation being tracked.
- **Floor- or wall-referenced**, so the number repeats. Already how the assessment is built.
- **Re-test on the same schedule**, not when someone feels flexible.

## 10. What a flexibility training session actually contains

Section 8 records that **no mobility training session exists yet - only the assessment** - and that
the exercises get built from real numbers rather than guessed in advance. **That still stands and
this section does not break it.** What follows is the *shape* of the session and the rules for
filling it, so that when the assessment numbers arrive the session can be written in one sitting
instead of researched again.

### The prescription, restated as a checklist

From sections 4, 5 and 6, which already contain everything needed:

1. **One or two skills at a time**, not eight. Daniel has chosen **pike + deep squat** as the first
   pair (section 8).
2. **3-5 stretches per session**, all aimed at the same skill.
3. **Ordered small and isolated first, compound last** - the reverse of a lifting session. Each one
   opens range for the next.
4. **1-2 sessions a week.**
5. **Alternate assisted and resisted work** across the spectrum in section 5, so range and strength
   in that range advance together.
6. **Isometric holds for reps** - about 3 seconds per rep with short rests - rather than one long
   hold. Named in section 5 as one of the most frequently programmed methods.

### The scheduling decision, which is the one that is still open

Section 6 ranks the options and the ranking is the part most people get wrong:

1. **Best:** 2+ days after the strength or sport work that loads the same area.
2. **Good:** same day, strength in the morning and flexibility in the evening.
3. **Acceptable:** straight after the strength session, with both trimmed a little.
4. **Worst:** the day after strength training, or parked on a rest day as "active recovery".

**Applied to their actual week, this is a real constraint and not a formality.** Pike and deep squat
both load the posterior chain, adductors and calves, which is exactly what Lower 1 and Lower 2 load.
So the flexibility session must not land the day after a Lower day, which is the slot it would
naturally fall into and the slot that ranks worst.

The Tuesday rest and mobility day is the obvious candidate and is **exactly the trap section 6
describes** if it sits the day after a lower-body session. Where it goes depends on the week's shape
and on the uni timetable, which is why section 8 lists it as open. **It is a scheduling decision with
a right answer, not a preference.**

### What still cannot be written yet

The exercises. They come from the assessment, because the whole method is that you train your lowest
rung and nobody knows yet what either of theirs is. Guessing would produce a generic routine, which
is the exact failure mode section 1 exists to prevent.

## 11. What Hyrox actually demands, mobility-wise

`hyrox-method.md` is the event document. This section is the overlap: the restrictions that
specifically cost time or cause no-reps in the eight stations, and how they map onto the ladder we
already assess.

| Station | Needs | What a restriction costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Wall balls** | Ankle dorsiflexion, hip flexion, thoracic extension - all three together | Reduced squat depth, forward torso collapse, lumbar strain, and **no-repped reps**, which is the most expensive failure in the race |
| **Sled push** | Ankle dorsiflexion, hip extension | Ankle stiffness prevents the forward lean and the traction that make the push work; restricted hip extension makes the low back compensate |
| **Sandbag lunges** | Hip-flexor length on the trailing leg, hip flexion on the front | The **knee-to-floor standard** becomes hard to hit and hold; the trailing leg will not extend and the front hip will not open |
| **Running** | Hip extension | Stride length shortens |
| **SkiErg** | Thoracic extension | Overhead force transfer becomes inefficient |

**Three observations worth carrying forward:**

1. **Ankle dorsiflexion appears in three of the five rows.** Knee-to-wall is already on our
   assessment - added deliberately as a departure from the source (section 8) because it is the most
   common hard stop on squat depth. That decision looks better now than when it was made: it is also
   the most common hard stop on the Hyrox stations.
2. **The deep squat skill Daniel already chose is the single most Hyrox-relevant one available.** It
   feeds wall balls directly and the sled push through the ankle. It was chosen for stacking reasons
   rather than Hyrox reasons and it happens to be right for both.
3. **Hip extension is not currently assessed.** It appears in two rows above - sled push and running
   stride - and neither the pike nor the deep-squat cluster tests it. **Adding a hip-extension
   measure is the one gap in the assessment that this research pass found.** It is not urgent, since
   there is no race date, and it is a program change rather than a coaching note - so it goes through
   `propose_suggestion_tool` if it is wanted.

**What this does not change:** nothing in section 6 or in `coaching-method.md` Part 3. Flexibility
work is still training rather than recovery, it still does not go back into a cool-down because a
station needs it, and the removals listed in the coaching method stay removed.
