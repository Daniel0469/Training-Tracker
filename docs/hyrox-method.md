# Part 1 - The event

Eight 1 km runs, each followed by one station, in a fixed order. You always run first.

| # | Run | Then station | Standard |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 km | SkiErg | 1000 m |
| 2 | 1 km | Sled push | 50 m (4 x 12.5 m) |
| 3 | 1 km | Sled pull | 50 m (4 x 12.5 m) |
| 4 | 1 km | Burpee broad jumps | 80 m |
| 5 | 1 km | Row | 1000 m |
| 6 | 1 km | Farmers carry | 200 m |
| 7 | 1 km | Sandbag lunges | 100 m |
| 8 | 1 km | Wall balls | 100 reps |

That is **8 km of running** and eight stations, and the clock never stops.

**The RoxZone** is the timed transition corridor between a run and a station, and between a station
and the next run. It is part of your total, and it is where recreational athletes leak the most
time for the least reason. At an intermediate finish it is around 7:30 across the whole race,
roughly 56 seconds per transition. Elite teams get the whole thing under 5 minutes. None of that
requires fitness - it requires knowing where you are going and not standing still.

**2026/27 rule changes worth knowing:**

- Every station must be completed in full. Failure to complete one is a disqualification, with no
  partial credit.
- A sandbag drop is a **15-second penalty**, not an instant disqualification as in earlier seasons.
  More forgiving, but there are no warnings.
- The doubles togetherness gap tightened to **10 seconds** (see Part 2).

# Part 2 - Doubles, and specifically mixed doubles

## What is shared and what is not

**Both athletes run all eight kilometres.** There is no splitting the running, no pacing
separately, no one partner resting while the other runs. If one of you slows down, you both slow
down.

**Station work is split however you like.** There is no rule requiring an even split. The only
requirement is that the total reps or distance are completed before the team leaves the station.

**The togetherness standard is 10 seconds**, measured by the official timing equipment as you move
in and out of the RoxZones. You cannot start a station until you have reunited. It tightened from
15 seconds for the 2026/27 season, explicitly to make doubles a team event rather than two people
sharing a bib.

This is the single most consequential fact in this document.

## The arithmetic that decides our training

For a solo athlete at a 1:30 finish, the time divides roughly: **49% running, 40% stations, 8%
transitions**.

In doubles, the station work halves but **the running does not change at all**. Run the same numbers
through a pair and the split becomes roughly **60-65% running**. Doubles teams finish about 7-9
minutes faster than solo athletes on median precisely because they do four stations' worth of work
and still cover the full 8 km.

**So in doubles, running is not one of the things you train. It is the thing you train.** Every
second you take off a kilometre is worth eight times that in race time, and there is no partner to
hide behind.

## The peer-reviewed version of the same conclusion

The arithmetic above is derived from race-result blogs. There is also a **peer-reviewed study**
(Frontiers in Physiology, 2025) that reaches the same place by a completely different route, and it
is worth quoting properly because it is the only primary research this document set has on Hyrox.

Eleven recreational athletes (median 18 months of Hyrox experience, median VO2max 51 mL/min/kg) did
a lab pre-test and then a simulated Individual Open race, with heart rate throughout and blood
lactate and RPE after every run and every station.

**Median finish 86.5 minutes: 51.2 minutes running, 32.8 minutes on the stations.** Even in the
individual division, running was **59% of the moving time**.

What correlated with a faster finish:

| Predicted a faster time | |
|---|---|
| VO2max | ρ = -0.71, p = 0.01 |
| Endurance training volume | ρ = -0.68, p = 0.04 |
| Lower body fat % | ρ = 0.67, p = 0.03 |

What did **not**:

| No relationship with finish time | |
|---|---|
| Hand grip strength | p = 0.79 |
| Resistance training volume | p = 0.31 |
| Muscle mass % | p = 0.94 |

Correlations appeared **only for the runs, not for the stations**. The authors conclude Hyrox is an
endurance-focused modality with **moderate-to-low maximum-strength requirements**, and recommend
prioritising endurance training with concurrent-training principles applied to limit interference.

**Two caveats, stated plainly.** n = 11 is a small sample, and these are correlations rather than
causes. Read the direction, not the precision.

**One genuinely counter-intuitive finding worth carrying:** the stations provoked *higher* intensity
than the runs - max lactate 8.5 vs 7.7 mmol/L, max RPE 18 vs 16, average HR 173.7 vs 168.9 - and
79.5% of the race was spent at "very hard" intensity. **The stations feel harder and the runs decide
your time.** That gap between what hurts and what matters is exactly the trap this document exists
to keep us out of.

See `strength-method.md` Part 4 for what this means for how much strength work is actually
warranted. Short version: strength for Hyrox is a **sufficiency** problem, not a maximisation one.

## The mismatched-pair problem, and the one elegant fix

The published guidance is that partners work best within 10-15 s/km of each other, and that beyond
about a 15-20% fitness gap the pairing starts to cost real time: the faster runner is held back and
arrives at transitions rested, while the slower runner is pushed past comfortable pace and arrives
already gassed.

Daniel and Cerys are well outside that band today (Part 3). There are exactly two levers:

1. **Close the gap** - which mostly means the slower runner's running improves, and which is the
   real work.
2. **Equalise the fatigue at the stations**, which is the clever bit and is free.

The second one deserves stating properly, because it is the most useful single tactic in this
document:

> **The stronger runner should take more of the station work, and specifically should take the END
> of each station**, so that the pair leaves the RoxZone with the faster runner tired and the slower
> runner comparatively fresh. You are deliberately spending the faster athlete's surplus on the
> stations so it does not go to waste standing around, and so that the run - the only part that
> cannot be split - starts with both people closer to the same state.

Applied to us: Daniel takes the larger share of the sled, the ski erg, the burpees and the closing
reps of everything, and Cerys arrives at each run less cooked than she otherwise would. It does not
fix a large pace gap, but it converts Daniel's spare capacity into something useful.

The corollary from the same sources: **the slower runner needs to train at the faster runner's tempo
sometimes.** Running only at your own comfortable pace never rehearses the actual race demand, which
is holding someone else's pace.

## Splitting each station

Two schools, and they disagree. The data-led guidance favours **high-frequency switching** - short
work intervals, near-maximal effort, micro-recovery while your partner works - and finds 50/50
single-changeover splits slower because of the lactate cost of long work intervals. The tactical
guides favour **minimising changeovers** because each swap costs seconds. Both are right about
different stations: swap often where the limiter is local muscular fatigue, swap rarely where the
limiter is grip or where the swap itself is slow.

A defensible starting set, to be tested rather than assumed:

| Station | Split | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| SkiErg 1000 m | 500/500, or alternate every 100-250 m | Upper-body and aerobic. Frequent swaps keep power high. |
| Sled push 50 m | 12.5 m blocks; stronger athlete takes 30-35 m | Leg-destroying. Frequency protects the next run. |
| Sled pull 50 m | 12.5 m blocks; stronger athlete takes more | Same, plus grip. |
| Burpee broad jumps 80 m | 3-4 switches, 2-5 reps a turn, called live | Fatigue arrives unpredictably. Do not pre-plan rigidly. |
| Row 1000 m | 500/500, one changeover | The changeover is slow (seat, straps). Minimise it. |
| Farmers carry 200 m | 100 m each, swap at the turn | Grip fatigue does not carry into later stations. Go long. |
| Sandbag lunges 100 m | 3-4 switches | Quad-limited. But do not over-swap: momentum costs. |
| Wall balls 100 reps | Alternate sets of 10, or 60/40 | Your partner's set is your rest. The split makes this station easy. |

**Fumbled transitions cost 30-60 seconds per station.** That is 4-8 minutes across a race, for
free, and it is the cheapest thing on this whole list to fix. Rehearse changeovers as their own
drill until they are wordless.

**Communication protocol:** short, agreed, boring. "Switch", "stay", "last five". Encouragement
during work, status checks between stations, strategy only in the RoxZone.

# Part 4 - The eight stations

Each entry: what it is, what it actually demands, the technique that matters, and what to do with
the kit they have.

## 1. SkiErg, 1000 m

**Damper is set to 6 for all divisions**, and you may change it during the race as often as you
like. Benchmarks: roughly 4:30-6:00 recreational, 3:30-4:30 intermediate, around 3:00 (men) or 3:25
(women) elite.

**Technique.** Stand under the handles, feet about shoulder width, slight forward lean through the
ankles so the **hip hinge starts each stroke**. The stroke is hips, then core, then arms - not arms.
Keep the legs in tension as a base. Control the return; do not let the handles fly back.

**Common errors:** pulling down toward the floor instead of back past the hips; damper set too high,
which feels powerful and destroys pacing; arms-only stroking.

**Pacing:** 80-85% effort for the first 500-600 m, hold, then lift over the last 400-500 m.

**Trainable directly** if the ski erg is the machine they have. If it is only a rower, the rower
trains the same aerobic system but not the overhead position or the hinge pattern - lat pulldowns
and straight-arm pulldowns cover the pattern.

## 2. Sled push, 50 m at 152 kg

The station that punishes untrained people hardest, and the one where mixed doubles hurts most.

**Technique.** Body angle is the whole game: **a shallow angle, torso around 30-45 degrees, hips
below shoulders**, so your force goes horizontally into the sled rather than down into the floor.
Two arm positions work - forearms hugged onto the posts with elbows tight, which keeps the centre of
mass low, or arms slightly bent and held rigid. Never let the elbows collapse or the wrists break;
that absorbs force you meant for the sled.

**Fast feet, not big steps.** Long powerful strides work for about 10 m at 152 kg and then you stop.
Small quick steps sustain power.

**Start explosively** by leaning your weight into it, because static friction is the expensive part.
Same reason you should never let it fully stop mid-lane.

**Trainable directly** - they have a sled. Build to race weight over months, not weeks. If the sled
is ever unavailable, the closest substitutes are deadmill pushes (belt off, push the belt), banded
sprints, and loaded incline walking at high grade.

## 3. Sled pull, 50 m at 103 kg

Rope, hand over hand, **standing - you may not pull from seated or kneeling** in the race, even
though seated rope pulls are a fine training exercise.

**Technique.** Hips down and back, chest up, athletic position. **The hands never both arrive at the
hip together** - as one lands the other is already reaching. Long greedy pulls beat lots of little
tugs. Keep tension on the rope so the sled never stops (static friction again). **Walk backwards to
the line between pulls** rather than shuffling - every metre you cover on your feet is rope you do
not have to pull.

**Trainable directly.** Grip endurance is the co-limiter: farmers walks at about 50% bodyweight per
hand, 3 x 40 m at the end of upper-body sessions, is the standard prescription.

## 4. Burpee broad jumps, 80 m

No kit, no load, and the most divisive station.

**Three ways to do it:** jump (fastest, most cardiovascularly expensive), step up (slower per rep,
much cheaper), or a mix - jumping early and switching to stepping as fatigue arrives. **Stepping up
is more efficient for most people.** The most efficient jump variant is a low frog jump: two-footed
takeoff, travel horizontally, minimal height, clean landing, chain the next rep immediately.

**Pacing:** find a rhythm you can hold for all 80 m and lock it in from rep one. Big early jumps
reliably produce a stall.

**Trainable directly**, anywhere, and it is the station where technique choice buys the most time
for the least fitness.

## 5. Row, 1000 m

**Damper 4-6**, with 4-5 usually the best trade of power against leg cost - and leg cost matters
because there are three more runs after this.

**Stroke rate 24-28 spm** with a full, strong drive: legs, then body swing, then arms into the lower
ribs. **Even pacing across the full 1000 m** is the reliable strategy; going out hard in the first
200 m costs more later than it saves.

**Trainable directly.**

## 6. Farmers carry, 200 m at 2 x 24 kg

A grip-endurance test at a moderate load, not a strength test.

**Technique.** Neutral wrists, squeeze from the base of the palm, and **do not death-grip** - you
cannot hold a maximal grip for 200 m. Tall posture. Use the turnarounds to breathe and reset.

**Strategy: target unbroken.** Every drop costs 10-15 seconds minimum, and steady beats
walk/drop/pickup cycles. If you must break, one planned stop at 100 m beats three panicked ones.

**Trainable directly** with dumbbells or kettlebells. This is also the station where the doubles
split is most generous: 100 m each at the turn, and grip fatigue does not carry forward into lunges
or wall balls.

## 7. Sandbag lunges, 100 m at 20 kg

**Movement standard: the trailing knee must touch the floor** each rep.

**Technique.** Torso vertical, chest up, core braced - think about driving the sternum up as you
stand. **Push through the heel of the front foot**, not the toe: heel drive recruits glutes and
hamstrings and **preserves the quads for the wall balls that are still to come**. Small-to-medium
steps so you can place the knee precisely. Land soft and control the descent.

**Pacing, and this one is counter-intuitive:** the data says athletes who attack the lunges do
**not** run slower afterwards than athletes who pace them, and pacing conservatively costs an
estimated 30-90 seconds for no benefit. Elites go unbroken; recreational athletes should too. It is
only 100 m.

**Trainable directly** - they have sandbags. Mobility co-requisite: the knee-to-floor standard needs
hip-flexor length on the trailing leg and hip flexion on the front, which is exactly the
`flexibility-method.md` territory.

## 8. Wall balls, 6 kg, 100 reps

The last station, done on completely empty legs, and the one that decides more races than any other.
Cerys throws to 2.74 m, Daniel to 3.00 m.

**Technique.** **It is a squat, not a throw.** The ball comes off the leg drive; the arms only guide
it. Keep the ball resting close to the chest at the catch rather than held out in front, which burns
the shoulders. Catch soft and ride it straight back down into the next rep, using the momentum
rather than fighting it. **If you do not hit depth, or do not hit the line, the rep does not count**
and you do it again - late in a race that is the most expensive mistake available.

**Strategy: break it up from rep one, on a plan you decided in advance.** A big unbroken opening set
feels heroic and reliably stalls around rep 40. Workable patterns: 15-10-10-10-10-10-10-10-5, or 10
x 10, or 25-15-10-5. Short standing rests of 5-10 seconds, taken on schedule rather than at failure.

**In doubles this station gets much easier** - alternating sets of 10 means your partner's set is
your rest, and it stops being the wall it is in singles.

**Substitutes if there is no wall ball.** Nothing replicates it exactly, and this is the one station
worth finding a way to practise before race day. Closest options, in order: a medicine ball thrown to
a marked height on any solid wall (a chalk or tape line at 2.74 m or 3.00 m is all it takes); a
dumbbell or goblet thruster for the squat-to-overhead pattern and the metabolic cost; front squats
for the leg endurance. The two things a substitute must preserve are **squat depth** and **the
catch-to-squat rhythm** - a thruster gives you the first but not the second.

# Part 5 - Pacing and benchmarks

## What the finish times mean

Mixed doubles benchmarks (2026 data, 425,000+ results):

| Level | Mixed doubles finish |
|---|---|
| Elite, top 10% | 58-68 min |
| Competitive, top 25% | 68-78 min |
| Above average, top 50% | 78-88 min |
| Average recreational | 88-98 min |

Open doubles median across all pairs is **1:21:59**. Mixed pairs typically fall between men's and
women's pairs, and **performance is determined by the slower partner's station work** and, more
sharply, by the slower partner's run pace.

## The split model

For a solo 1:30 finish, the shape looks like this. It is worth reading not for the numbers
themselves but for the **run-by-run progression**:

| Run | Split | Station | Split |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4:00 | SkiErg | 5:00 |
| 2 | 5:20 | Sled push | 3:10 |
| 3 | 5:40 | Sled pull | 4:50 |
| 4 | 5:40 | Burpee broad jumps | 5:00 |
| 5 | 5:45 | Row | 5:00 |
| 6 | 5:45 | Farmers carry | 2:30 |
| 7 | 5:50 | Sandbag lunges | 4:40 |
| 8 | 6:00 | Wall balls | 6:00 |
| **Total** | **44:00** | **Total** | **36:10** |

RoxZone 7:30. Running 49%, stations 40%, transitions 8%.

**Run 8 is always slower than run 1.** That is not a pacing failure, it is what glycogen depletion
and cumulative fatigue do. But note the size of it: 4:00 to 6:00 is a 50% slowdown, and **most of
that gap is created by run 1 being far too fast**. The single most common recreational error in
Hyrox is running the first kilometre at a pace justified by nothing except fresh legs.

**Our version of the rule:** run 1 should feel almost embarrassingly controlled. In doubles it is
worse, because two people egg each other on. Agree run 1's target pace out loud before the start and
hold each other to it.

This connects directly to the race-pacing framework already in `coaching-method.md` Part 1: km 3 and
4 are where a 5k is won. In Hyrox the equivalent is runs 5 through 7, and the way you win them is by
not spending them in run 1.

## Target pace

The established rule of thumb: **Hyrox run pace = 15-20 minute time-trial pace + 10-15 s/km**, to
account for station fatigue and the stop-start. For a pair, use the **slower** partner's number and
add nothing extra - the pair pace is already the compromise.

# Part 6 - The phase model

No date is booked, so this is written as phases with durations. **When a date exists, anchor
backwards from it**: taper, then sharpen, then specific, then build, then base, and whatever is left
over is base.

The published beginner plans are 12 weeks and assume you can already run the distance. **We cannot
use one.** What follows is longer at the front and shorter at the back for exactly that reason.

## Phase 0 - Prerequisites (as long as it takes)

**Nothing Hyrox-specific happens until these are true.** They are not negotiable and they are the
honest reason this is not a twelve-week project.

- **Cerys can run 20 minutes continuously, pain-free**, having built there through the MTSS
  return-to-run sequence in `coaching-method.md`. Today her longest continuous run is about a
  minute. This is the single longest item on the list.
- **Daniel can run 8 km continuously**, at any pace. Today his longest is 3.45 km.
- Both are running **at least twice a week**, which they currently are not - see `running-method.md`
  for why one run a week cannot get either of them there.
- Cerys is comfortable under the **mixed-doubles station loads**, or the plan for who takes what is
  built around her not being.

Phase 0 is the phase we are in. It is worth saying so rather than pretending otherwise.

## Phase 1 - Base (8-12 weeks)

Aerobic volume and station technique, at light loads.

- Running volume up, intensity mostly easy. Polarised distribution (see `running-method.md`).
- Every station learned and practised **for technique**, at loads well under race weight. The point
  is that no movement is unfamiliar, not that any of it is hard yet.
- Strength work continues as normal. It is not competing with this phase; heavy lifting improves
  running economy (again, `running-method.md`).
- Transitions rehearsed as a drill, because they are free time and need no fitness.

## Phase 2 - Build (6-8 weeks)

Race-weight loads and running intervals arrive.

- Station loads step up toward race weight. Sled push especially: build to 152 kg gradually.
- Threshold running becomes the main quality session.
- First **compromised-running** sessions: one station, then a run. Keep them short.
- Grip work as a named priority, because sled pull and farmers carry sit six stations apart and both
  need it.

## Phase 3 - Specific (6-8 weeks)

The phase that actually makes you good at Hyrox rather than good at its components.

- **Compromised running 2-3 times a week.** The standard prescription: 4-6 rounds of 800 m to 1 km
  at goal race pace, each immediately after one leg-heavy station (20-30 sandbag lunges, or 10
  burpee broad jumps, or a sled length), with 1-2 minutes between rounds to mimic a transition.
  **Minimise the pause between the station and the run** - any rest reduces how compromised you
  actually are, which is the whole point.
- **Pair rehearsal.** Splits, changeovers, and the calls, at race intensity. This is where you find
  out that your agreed wall-ball split does not survive contact.
- Cerys runs at Daniel's tempo in some sessions and Daniel runs at Cerys's in others.

## Phase 4 - Sharpen and simulate (3-4 weeks)

- **Half simulations** (4 runs, 4 stations) before any full one.
- One full race simulation, no earlier than 3 weeks out, ideally at the real loads.
- Race-day fuelling rehearsed in a simulation, not invented on the day - see `fuelling-method.md`.

## Taper (1-2 weeks)

Volume down, intensity retained, loads down. The standard endurance taper: keep the sharpness, lose
the fatigue.

## Anchoring rule

If the runway is short, **cut Phase 1 and 2, never Phase 0 and never Phase 3.** Turning up
underprepared aerobically is a slow race; turning up having never run compromised is a bad one; and
turning up on shins that were not ready is an injury.

- **The mixed-doubles load table has not been read off the official rulebook** (Part 2), though
  Daniel's own check agrees with it. Re-check for whichever season they enter.
- **No race date, no venue, no target time.** Every number in Part 5 is a benchmark, not a target,
  until Daniel picks one.
- **No wall ball, so that station will be trained by substitute** until a medicine ball and a marked
  wall exist. How much that costs on the day is unknown and probably small, but it is not zero.
- **Neither of them has done any station except running**, so every station estimate here is generic
  rather than theirs. The first time they touch a sled at load, this file gets better.
- **Cerys has one bodyweight on record** (79.8 kg, 1 Aug) and no wellness data exists for either of
  them, so the fuelling and recovery documents are working from principles rather than measurements.
