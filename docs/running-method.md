# Running method - the week around the session, and the runner in it

**Written 2026-09-02.**

`coaching-method.md` Part 1 covers **the single session**: which type to prescribe at which level,
current-versus-goal pace, recovery type, and race pacing. It is good on that and none of it is
repeated here.

This file covers everything around it - **how many runs a week, how much total, at what mix of
intensities, and what makes a runner faster other than running.** It exists because the existing
method could tell you exactly what one good session looks like and had nothing to say about the
fact that one session a week is the whole of their running.

---

# Part 1 - The one-run-a-week problem

## What they actually do

Both Daniel and Cerys run **once a week**. That is the entire running programme. Around it sit four
lifting days and, for Cerys, incline walking.

## What the evidence says that buys you

Bluntly: not much, and honesty here is worth more than encouragement.

- **One run a week is enough to complete a 5k**, and no more than that.
- **To improve sustainably you need roughly 15-20 km a week across at least three sessions.**
- Two to four runs a week of 30-75 minutes each is the range in which a recreational runner can
  perform well over 5k. Three is the commonly cited floor for actual progression.
- Below that, the stimulus is too infrequent for the adaptations to accumulate faster than they
  decay between sessions.

There is a real "sub-20 on three runs a week" tradition in coaching, and it works - but note that it
is *three*, and it assumes a base that already exists.

## What that means for their goals

- **Daniel's sub-20 5k** (4:00/km against a current 5:12/km estimate) is not reachable on one run a
  week. It is not close. The gap is not speed - he has held 13 km/h reps at 79% of max HR - it is
  that nothing in his week builds the ability to hold anything for twenty minutes.
- **Hyrox doubles is 8 km of running**, run as eight separate kilometres, and the pair pace is set by
  the slower partner. See `hyrox-method.md` Part 2. One run a week does not build that for either of
  them.
- **Cerys's stated goal is the pull-up**, not a run time, and that is worth respecting. But the
  Hyrox goal she also holds requires 8 km of running from someone whose longest continuous run is
  currently about a minute.

**So the highest-value single change available to either of them is a second run a week.** Not a
harder run, not a better-designed run. A second one.

## Why this is a genuinely hard change, and not just an oversight

It would be dishonest to write "just run more" and stop. The reasons they run once a week are real:

- **Cerys's shins are the governor.** More running is exactly the thing that has flared them three
  times. Part 2 is about how to add frequency without adding impact.
- **Five lifting-and-cardio days already exist**, plus a rest/mobility day. There is no obviously
  free slot, and adding one costs something.
- **Daniel's sessions already overrun** and he has repeated notes of ending early tired or hungry.
  Adding load to a week that is already producing those notes is not automatically right - see
  `fuelling-method.md` and `recovery-method.md`, which between them cover the likelier explanation.

The conclusion is not "add a run tomorrow". It is that **the running frequency is the constraint on
both of their stated goals, and every other running decision is second-order until it changes.**
Whether and how to change it is Daniel's call, and it belongs in a proposal rather than in a coach's
note.

# Part 2 - Adding running without adding impact

## What cross-training actually does

The evidence here is less flattering than the fitness industry suggests, and the distinction matters
enormously for Cerys.

- **Cross-training maintains the engine.** Cycling and elliptical groups maintain VO2max. Four weeks
  of elliptical training produced the same physiological and performance results as running in one
  study. Both cycling and elliptical improved 3000 m performance in others.
- **Cross-training does not reliably improve running performance.** A recent systematic review and
  meta-analysis of running-cycling cross-training found no clear improvement or decrement in
  sport-specific VO2max or running performance over short to moderate periods. In one study cycling
  improved VO2max but **reduced running economy**, netting out to no change in performance.
- **Fidelity ranking**, best to worst: deep-water running and elliptical (similar gait, similar
  central load), then cycling and rowing (different gait, high cardiovascular ceiling).

**The rule that falls out of this:** cross-training preserves the engine. **It does not build the
tissue.** Bone, tendon and the specific coordination of running only adapt to running. So for
Cerys, incline walking and the erg are not a substitute for the return-to-run sequence - they are
what keeps her fit *while* she does it.

This also reframes the ski erg and rower in `hyrox-method.md`. They are not just station practice;
they are aerobic volume that costs her shins nothing.

## Frequency before duration, for bone especially

The most useful shape for adding running to a shin-limited runner is **more, shorter runs** rather
than one longer one. Bone responds to loading cycles and needs recovery between them; three
twenty-minute runs load the tibia three times with two full recoveries, where one hour-long run
loads it once, hard, at the end when form is worst.

This also sits well with the single-session spike finding in Part 6, which says the dangerous thing
is a long run that is much longer than your recent longest - not the weekly total.

## The specific case: Zone 2 for someone who cannot run slowly

Cerys has stated it herself and the data agrees: **Zone 2 is a walk for her, not a run.** She is in
Zone 4 within a minute of starting to run, even at an easy 10 km/h. `coaching-method.md` Part 3
already carries the override - never prescribe her a low-HR run, and incline walking at 4-6% is how
she gets Zone 2, reliably at HR around 139 and genuinely pain-free.

The addition this document makes is the *reason*: her aerobic base is being built by the incline
walking, and that is legitimate and is working. What the incline walking cannot do is prepare her
tibia for 8 km of running. Those are two different projects and both need doing.

# Part 3 - Intensity distribution

## The two models

- **Polarised:** roughly 75-85% of running at low intensity, about 5% at threshold, 15-20% hard.
  Often shorthanded as 80/20.
- **Threshold:** only about 35-55% low intensity, with much more time in the moderate-to-threshold
  middle.

## What the evidence says

Meta-analytic results favour polarised for time-trial performance. In a direct 10-week comparison in
recreational runners both models improved 10 km time, with polarised better (-5% against -3.5%) -
but **the authors themselves concluded the overall evidence is not strong enough to declare a
winner.** Take that seriously: this is a genuine "probably, slightly" rather than a settled result.

The clearer argument for polarised at low volume is the failure mode it prevents. Threshold-heavy
and high-volume models are the ones associated with overreaching and burnout across all levels.

## How this reconciles with the existing method

`coaching-method.md` says that with **one run per week, threshold is the default** - the
highest-yield single session, and the thing that stops the aerobic base eroding. That is correct
and it should not change.

**The two are not in conflict; they answer different questions.** Intensity distribution is a
statement about a week containing several runs. With one run, there is no distribution to
polarise - you have one session and you should spend it on the highest-yield thing, which is
threshold.

The moment a second run exists, the distribution question becomes real, and the answer is: **the
new run is an easy one.** Not a second quality session. The commonest error when a runner goes from
one run to two is to make both of them hard, which produces the worst version of the threshold model
- all moderate, no easy, no genuinely hard.

For Cerys specifically, "easy" is where the incline-walking override bites: her easy day is
non-impact, and the run she does is the quality one. That is an unusual distribution and it is the
right one while tissue tolerance is the governor.

# Part 4 - Running economy, and why the lifting is not a distraction

This is the most useful thing in this document, and it reverses an assumption that is easy to make
about a programme with four lifting days and one run.

## What the meta-analyses show

- **Strength training significantly improves running economy** against controls.
- **Heavy resistance training produces a larger effect than plyometric training** overall, and
  **heavy loads at or above 90% of 1RM produce greater effects than lighter loads.**
- Both heavy-load training alone and heavy-load combined with plyometrics improved running economy
  **and 5 km performance.**
- The effects are speed-dependent: **plyometrics improve economy at speeds at or below 12 km/h**,
  while **high-load strength work is particularly effective at higher running speeds.**
- Duration matters: greater effects at 10-14 weeks for heavy resistance work and 8-10 weeks for
  plyometrics. This is a months-long adaptation, not a block you do for three weeks.

The overall magnitude is honestly described as small-to-moderate. It is not a substitute for running.
But it is real, it is repeatedly replicated, and **they are already doing the intervention.**

## What that means for these two

**For Daniel, the barbell goals and the sub-20 are the same project, not competing ones.** A 100 kg
bench, a 200 kg squat and a 200 kg deadlift are pursued through exactly the heavy, near-maximal
loading that the evidence says improves running economy at the faster speeds he runs at. He has been
worrying, implicitly, about a trade-off that the literature says is mostly a synergy.

**For Cerys, the speed-dependence points at plyometrics** - she runs at around 10 km/h, which is
inside the band where plyometric training is the more effective intervention for economy.

**But that recommendation collides directly with her shins, and the shins win.** Plyometrics are
high-impact by definition, and MTSS is a tissue-tolerance problem. So:

- **Do not prescribe plyometrics to Cerys while shin pain is live**, however good the economy
  argument is. This is exactly the kind of case `coaching-method.md` Part 3 exists to catch.
- The heavy-load route is available to her instead, is lower impact, and is the direction her
  strength work is already going.
- If and when she is fully clear of shin symptoms for a sustained block, low-volume plyometrics
  become a reasonable proposal - through `propose_suggestion_tool`, and starting far lower than
  feels necessary.

## The interference effect, since it is the obvious objection

The classic worry is that running blunts strength gains. The current position:

- **Three recent meta-analyses found concurrent training did not significantly compromise strength
  or hypertrophy** compared with lifting alone.
- Where interference does appear, it is **driven by total load exceeding recovery capacity**, not by
  a metabolic conflict between pathways. It correlates with endurance frequency and duration.
- **Running interferes more than cycling** in at least one review, though other work found the
  opposite, so treat modality as a weak moderator and total load as the strong one.
- **Untrained and early-intermediate lifters show essentially no interference** and gain from both.
- **Same-day order:** if both happen in one day, resistance work first supports lower-body strength;
  if hard endurance is the priority, some guidance reverses it. **Separate the two by at least three
  hours** where possible, and the effect largely disappears.

**Applied here:** at one to two runs a week, interference is not a live concern for either of them.
It would become one at four or five runs a week alongside four heavy lifting days, which is not a
week anyone is proposing. If a second run is added, **put it on a day that is not adjacent to a
heavy lower-body session**, and the question closes.

# Part 5 - Cadence, and the one free lever for Cerys's shins

## The 180 spm myth

The number comes from Jack Daniels counting strides at the 1984 Olympics: of 46 elite distance
runners, only one was under 180. That is an observation of elites racing, not a universal target.
Recreational runners typically sit at 150-170. Optimal cadence depends on height, leg length, pace
and training history.

**What matters is the relative increase from your own baseline, not the absolute number.** A 5-10%
increase from a runner's self-selected cadence reduces ground reaction forces, knee and hip joint
loading, and overstriding - and it does so without the metabolic penalty that larger changes carry.
The protection comes from eliminating overstride, which happens at cadences well below 180.

## The tibial-load numbers, which are the reason this section exists

- A **10% cadence increase** reduced peak tibial acceleration by **11.5%**, vertical average loading
  rate by **15.6%**, vertical instantaneous loading rate by **13.5%**, and impulse by 3.5%.
- Across runners, **each additional step per minute is associated with about a 5% lower risk of bone
  stress injury.**
- The evidence is not unanimous - two studies found no clear directional effect of a 10% increase on
  tibial loading. So treat it as promising and cheap rather than proven.
- Alongside cadence, **reducing stride length and widening the base of support** also reduce tibial
  stress, and all three are standard components of gait retraining for MTSS.

## Their actual cadences, read correctly

`coaching-method.md` Part 1 already carries the rule: **never read whole-activity cadence.** A
recording containing a walk drags it to meaningless numbers - Daniel's read 116 once and he was told
to shorten his stride when his running cadence was fine.

Read per-rep, from the 29 July interval sessions:

- **Daniel: 149-156 spm** across six reps (and 157-166 elsewhere). Reasonable. Not the thing to fix.
- **Cerys: 140-151 spm** across five reps, trending down as she fatigued (151, 146, 148, 140, 144).

**Cerys is running at a genuinely low cadence, and she is the one with shin splints.** That is the
cleanest match between a lever and a problem in either of their data. A 5-10% increase would put her
around 148-166, which is unremarkable territory, and the tibial-load reduction is exactly the
mechanism her injury needs.

Two cautions before anyone acts on it:

1. **It is a proposal, not a coaching note.** It changes how she runs, which is a program-level
   change - `propose_suggestion_tool`, for Daniel to approve.
2. **The treadmill makes it easy to implement and easy to measure**, since belt speed is fixed:
   at a constant speed, more steps per minute *is* a shorter stride, mechanically, with nothing to
   get wrong. A metronome or the watch's cadence alert is the whole intervention.
3. **Her cadence falls as she fatigues** (151 down to 140). So the target is holding cadence late in
   a session, not hitting a number early.

# Part 6 - Progression rules

`coaching-method.md` already carries three: duration before intensity, hold a known speed while
volume climbs, and a completed rep range earns the next load. All three stand. Three more from the
load-management literature:

## The 10% rule is real, but not the one everyone quotes

An 18-month cohort of **5,205 runners and over half a million logged runs** (Aarhus University,
published in BJSM) found:

- **Weekly mileage totals and the acute:chronic workload ratio had little to no predictive value**
  for injury.
- **Single-run spikes did.** Comparing a run against the runner's own longest run in the previous 30
  days:

| Single-run increase | Injury risk |
|---|---|
| 10-30% longer | **+64%** |
| 30-100% longer | +52% |
| over 100% longer | **+128%** |

**The rule that survives:** do not increase your longest run by more than about 10% over your
furthest distance in the last 30 days. The weekly total is much less important than that one number.

This is directly actionable for both of them:

- **Daniel's longest run is 3.45 km.** Under this rule his next long run tops out around 3.8 km, then
  4.2, then 4.6. From 3.45 km to the 8 km a Hyrox needs is about **nine steps**, which at one long
  run a fortnight is four to five months, and at one a week is about two. That is a much less
  daunting number than it feels, and it is the argument for starting rather than waiting.
- **Cerys's rule is the MTSS return-to-run sequence**, which is stricter still and already written in
  `coaching-method.md`: hold rep length constant and add reps first, because rep length is what loads
  shins hardest. The two rules agree.

## The wider load-management picture

The acute:chronic workload ratio is widely used and **poorly supported** - the validity has been
questioned on the heterogeneity of the load measures used, thresholds and "sweet spots" have limited
evidence, and spikes in ACWR have been dissociated from injury occurrence in at least one
professional-sport cohort. **Do not build a rule on it.** Rapid load increases do raise injury risk;
that specific metric is not how to detect them.

# Part 7 - The treadmill, and what happens when they run outdoors

**Every kilometre either of them has ever run is on a belt.** Hyrox is not.

## Where Hyrox is actually run

Races take place **inside exhibition centres and arenas**, with the run course laid around the
workout zone. Each 1 km is **2-4 laps** depending on the venue - courses run roughly 250 m, 300 m or
500 m per lap - so a 1 km run contains several turns, and the exact count varies by venue and is
published on the race map. The running laps plus the RoxZone distance total about **8.7 km**.

So the demand is: a hard indoor floor, repeated turns, no belt, no display, and eight separate
efforts. None of which they have practised.

## The physiological difference is small

- Treadmill and overground running are **broadly comparable in both biomechanics and physiology**,
  per a 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis. Differences exist and are small - longer ground
  contact time on the belt in one review, and higher cadence with a shorter swing phase on the belt
  in another.
- The **1% incline convention** is contested. The original finding was that a 1% grade matched the
  oxygen cost of outdoor running at every speed tested, the reasoning being that outdoors you push a
  column of air out of the way and on a belt you do not. More recent work found **higher** oxygen
  uptake, breathing rate and heart rate on the treadmill than on a track at either gradient, and
  concluded a 1% incline only added to an already-higher demand. Another comparison put outdoor
  oxygen cost about **3.6% higher** than treadmill.

**Practical read:** the direction of the correction is disputed and the magnitude is a few percent
either way. **Not worth agonising over**, and certainly not worth more than the ~9% and ~13% watch
distance errors already documented in `coaching-method.md` Part 4. If a small incline is available
and they like it, fine. It is not a finding to build a prescription on.

## The surface difference is not small, and it matters for Cerys

**Running on a treadmill averaged 71.3% more shock absorption than running on asphalt.** Concrete is
harder still - the highest impact forces of any common surface, with asphalt about 10% softer.
Treadmills sit at the low-impact end alongside soft dirt and grass.

**Cerys's entire shin history has happened on the forgiving end of that range.** Medial tibial stress
syndrome is a bone-loading problem, and the surface she has been rehabilitating on absorbs most of
the load that a race floor will not.

This is the single most important thing in this section:

> **A first outdoor or race-floor run is a load increase even at identical pace and distance.** It
> does not show up in any number the watch records. Under the single-session spike rule in Part 6
> the distance would look safe; the tibial loading would not be.

The implication is not "avoid hard surfaces" - `recovery-method.md` Part 2 is explicit that graded
running is what strengthens the tibial cortex, and a race floor is what she will actually race on.
The implication is **introduce the surface as its own progression**, separately from and more slowly
than the distance progression, and not for the first time on race day.

## What outdoor running adds that a belt does not

- **You generate all the forward propulsion yourself.** The belt does not pull the leg through.
- **Wind resistance, terrain and surface texture vary**, so the foot, calf, quad, hamstring and glute
  have to keep adapting rather than repeating one stride.
- **Pacing is self-directed.** On a belt the speed is chosen once and then imposed; outdoors it is
  chosen continuously. That is a genuine skill and it is exactly what Hyrox demands - eight
  self-paced kilometres with no display telling you what you are doing.
- **Turns.** Two to four per kilometre in a race, each one a deceleration and re-acceleration.

## What this means for them

- **Daniel's programmed treadmill sequences are a real strength, not a limitation.** He explicitly
  wanted a session he does not have to adjust mid-run, and `coaching-method.md` Part 4 records that
  the belt takes the whole session as a program. Keep that for interval and threshold work; it is
  the most controllable way to train.
- **But a self-paced run has to exist somewhere**, because pacing is a trained skill and the belt
  removes the need for it entirely. This is an argument for the second run (Part 1) being the
  self-paced one.
- **For Cerys, the surface change is a load change** and belongs in the return-to-run planning, not
  as an afterthought.
- **Neither of them has ever run a self-paced kilometre**, let alone eight. That is worth knowing
  before any Hyrox time is predicted from treadmill numbers.
- **Do not convert treadmill paces to outdoor paces with a formula.** Between the disputed incline
  correction and the ~9%/~13% watch errors, the compounded uncertainty is larger than anything a
  conversion would tell you. Prefer a real outdoor run.
